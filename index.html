No gate. No test. No one true way.

“Pagan” is not one religion. It is a grove of many paths — and every path through it is walked by real people, in good faith.

Under this one word live earth-honoring traditions old and new: the Norse and Germanic ways, Wicca and witchcraft, Druidry, the gods of Greece and Egypt and Ireland, and countless folk practices carried quietly in families and kitchens. Some of us worship many gods, some one, some none at all — some honor the turning earth itself as sacred enough.

This site exists for all of us. You will not find a ranking of paths here, or a creed to sign. You will find the shared calendar that turns beneath most of our feet, honest introductions to each other's traditions, old wisdom worth carrying, and simple practices anyone can begin with — today, with nothing but a candle, a walk, and their attention.

New here and unsure how a word sounds? Pronunciations sit right beside the words that need them — for example, SamhainSOW-in, BlótBLOHT, and HávamálHAH-vah-mahl. No one in this grove will ever fault you for learning out loud.

The Wheel of the Year

Eight festivals mark the turning seasons — solstices, equinoxes, and the cross-quarter days between them. Not every pagan keeps all eight, and Heathens, Druids, and others keep their own calendars besides. But this wheel is the rhythm many of us share. Tap a festival to walk through its season. Dates below are for the Northern Hemisphere; in the Southern Hemisphere, the wheel turns six months opposite.

Many paths through the grove

Short, honest introductions — the way each tradition describes itself. If one of them pulls at you, follow it respectfully: read its own voices first, and take your time. There is no hurry on any of these roads.

Norse & Germanic Heathenry

ÁsatrúOW-sah-troo · Heathenry · Norse paganism

Devotion to the Norse gods — Odin, Thor, Freyr, Freyja, Frigg, and many more — and to ancestors and the spirits of the land. Heathenry is built on reciprocity: a gift for a gift. Its wellspring is the Poetic and Prose Eddas and the wisdom poem HávamálHAH-vah-mahl, and its heart is frith — the peace kept within family and community.

HallmarksBlótBLOHT (offering rite) and sumbelSOOM-bel (ritual toasting) · rune lore · hospitality as a sacred duty · honoring the ancestors by name

Wicca & Witchcraft

Wicca · Traditional & eclectic witchcraft

An earth-centered practice honoring the Goddess and the God, the moon's phases, and the eight sabbats of the Wheel. Wiccans work in covens or alone, cast circles, and practice magic as a craft of will and intention — bound, for many, by the ethic to harm none. Witchcraft beyond Wicca is older, wider, and gloriously varied.

HallmarksFull-moon esbatsESS-bats · circle casting · spellcraft · the altar as a working table of earth, air, fire, and water

Druidry

Modern Druid orders · Druidcraft

A spiritual path rooted in the lore of the ancient Celts and in love for the living earth. Modern Druids gather in groves, mark the eight festivals, and seek awenAH-wen — the flowing inspiration that moves through poetry, story, and craft. Many understand Druidry as a philosophy and a practice of peace as much as a religion.

HallmarksOutdoor ritual in groves · bardic arts — song, story, poetry · tree lore · meditation on the elements and the ancestors of the land

Celtic Polytheism

Irish, Welsh, Gaulish & Brythonic paths

Devotion to the gods of the Celtic peoples — the DagdaDAHG-dah, BrigidBREE-yid, LughLOO, the Morríganmor-REE-gan, Danu — grounded in the surviving myths, the Irish and Welsh texts, and careful reconstruction of old custom. Hospitality, honor, and right relationship with gods, ancestors, and land spirits run through all of it.

HallmarksOfferings at hearth and well · Brigid's cross at ImbolcIM-bolk · learning the languages and stories of the tradition itself

Hellenic Polytheism

Hellenismos · Olympian devotion

Worship of the Greek gods — Zeus, Hera, Athena, Apollo, Demeter, Dionysos, and the rest of Olympus — revived from ancient practice: household shrines, libations, festivals from the old Athenian calendar, and the virtue of xenia, sacred hospitality to the stranger. Kharis — grace given and returned — is its heartbeat.

HallmarksLibations of wine, milk, or water · household altar to Hestia and Zeus · monthly observances at the new moon · hymns of Homer and Orpheus

Kemetic Polytheism

Kemetic Orthodoxy · Egyptian devotion

Devotion to the Netjeru, the gods of ancient Egypt — Ra, Isis, Osiris, Bastet, Sekhmet, Thoth — ordered by ma'at: truth, balance, and right action. Daily shrine rites, offerings of bread and cool water, and care for the dead anchor a practice with five thousand years of memory behind it.

HallmarksMorning shrine rite · ma'at as daily ethics · honoring the akhu, the blessed dead · festival calendars tied to the Nile and the stars

Eclectic & Solitary Paths

Eclectic paganism · Hedgewitchery · Folk practice

Many pagans belong to no named tradition at all — and they are not lesser for it. The eclectic path draws honestly from many sources; the solitary keeps a private practice at a kitchen altar; the hedgewitch works with herbs, hedgerows, and household spirits. A path you walk with sincerity and care is a real path.

HallmarksA practice built by hand and by heart · kitchen and garden magic · journaling and personal gnosis, tested against tradition and common sense

Ritual recipes — begin where you stand

No tools to buy, no permission to seek. Each recipe takes about five minutes, start to finish: what you'll need, then the steps in order. Filter by the tradition you'd like to learn from — or keep “All paths” and borrow freely, with respect.

Tradition:
1

Keep a small altar

About 5 minutesGeneral · all paths

A shelf, a windowsill, a corner of a dresser. The altar isn't decoration — tending it for two minutes a day is the practice.

You'll need

  • A candle (or LED candle) for fire
  • A small bowl of water
  • One stone, leaf, or shell from a place you love
  • A cloth or tray to gather them on, if you like

Steps

  1. Choose and clear (1 min). Pick your spot and wipe it clean. Clearing the space is the first offering.
  2. Place the elements (1 min). Candle at the back, water beside it, your found object in front — earth, fire, and water in arm's reach.
  3. Light and name it (1 min). Light the candle and say, aloud or silently, what this altar is for: gratitude, ancestors, the season — one sentence is enough.
  4. Tend it (1 min). Straighten, refresh the water, remove anything dead or dusty. Small care, done daily, beats grand ritual done rarely.
  5. Close (1 min). One slow breath, a quiet “thank you,” and snuff — don't blow — the flame out.

Done when the spot feels claimed. Repeat daily; change the found object with the seasons.

2

Learn your land — the sit-spot walk

About 5 minutesCeltic · DruidryGeneral

Druids begin with the land they're actually on. Walk the same short route and watch it change — paganism starts with paying attention to the earth under your feet.

You'll need

  • One short route you can repeat — a block, a garden, a park path
  • Your phone or a notebook for three quick notes
  • No headphones

Steps

  1. Set out slowly (1 min). Walk at half your usual pace. Let your eyes go soft and wide.
  2. Name five things (2 min). Five trees, birds, or plants by name if you can — “oak,” “crow,” “clover.” Look up the ones you can't name when you get home.
  3. Read the season (1 min). What's budding, fruiting, falling, returning? Where's the frost, the first flower, the last leaf?
  4. Leave it better (1 min). Pick up one piece of litter, or move one fallen branch off the path. Attention with hands.

Same route, once a week. In a year you'll know your land the way you know a friend's face.

3

Make an offering — a small blótBLOHT

About 5 minutesNorse · HeathenGeneral

A gift for a gift — the oldest law of the grove. In Heathen practice this is a blót: food or drink given to gods, ancestors, or land spirits, and shared by the people present.

You'll need

  • A cup of something good — ale, juice, coffee, clean water
  • A small bite of food — bread is traditional
  • Your altar, a tree's roots, or a garden spot

Steps

  1. Settle (1 min). Stand at your spot, cup in hand, and let the day go quiet.
  2. Speak the gift (1 min). Say who it's for and why, in plain words: “For the ancestors, in thanks for —” and name one true thing.
  3. Pour and place (1 min). Pour half the drink at the roots or into a bowl on the altar; set the bread beside it. Outdoors, use only things the earth can take — no plastic, no glitter.
  4. Share (1 min). Drink the rest yourself. An offering shared is an offering completed.
  5. Close (1 min). “A gift for a gift.” Leave the food for the birds; pour bowl-water at a plant's roots later.

Small and sincere beats grand and rare. Once a week is a strong rhythm.

4

Honor your dead

About 5 minutesGeneral · all pathsNorseCeltic

Nearly every pagan tradition on earth begins here — the ancestors are the oldest altar there is. Say their names, and they stay part of the household.

You'll need

  • One photograph, or an object that was theirs
  • A candle
  • One story about them that you actually remember

Steps

  1. Set the place (1 min). Photo where you'll see it, candle beside it.
  2. Speak the names (1 min). Aloud, full names, one by one — family first, then ancestors of heart: teachers, friends, the ones who raised you in spirit.
  3. Tell one story (2 min). One true story, told well, to whoever's at your table — or to the empty room. Details matter: the laugh, the hands, the saying they always said.
  4. Thank and close (1 min). Thank them for one specific thing that reached you. Snuff the candle.

One story per season is eight ancestors kept alive a year at any table.

5

Full-moon esbatESS-bat — moon water & one intention

About 5 minutesWiccanGeneral

An esbat is the Wiccan observance of the moon, usually at the full moon — the monthly counterpart to the eight solar sabbats. The tracker at the top of this page tells you the next one. This is its smallest honest form.

You'll need

  • A clean jar or bottle of water
  • A windowsill or doorstep the moon can reach
  • Paper and pencil

Steps

  1. Go out and look (1 min). Find the moon first. Standing under it is step one, not a preliminary.
  2. Set the water (1 min). Place the jar where moonlight will fall on it overnight.
  3. Write one intention (2 min). One sentence, present tense, about what this moon cycle is for: “This cycle, I finish —” Keep it where you'll see it.
  4. Close the circle (1 min). Hands on the jar, one breath, “So it is.” In the morning, drink a sip or water a plant with it — intention, taken in.

Check the next esbat in the today bar above and put it on your calendar now, while you're thinking of it.

6

Keep the turning year

About 5 minutesGeneral · all paths

Eight small observances a year will teach you the shape of the seasons better than any book. This recipe is how you set the next one up — five minutes now, a festival kept later.

You'll need

  • The Wheel of the Year, above — and the next festival's date
  • Your calendar
  • One simple plan: a meal, a candle, or a sunset walk

Steps

  1. Find the next turning (1 min). Use the today bar: note the next festival and the next full moon, with dates.
  2. Put it on the calendar (1 min). Both of them, right now — a festival you have to remember is a festival you'll miss.
  3. Choose one small keeping (2 min). Pick a single way from the festival panel above — the dumb supper at SamhainSOW-in, seeds at Ostaraoh-STAR-ah, bread at LughnasadhLOO-nah-sah. One, done fully.
  4. Tell one person (1 min). “I'm keeping Yule with a candle at dusk — join me?” Festivals grow by invitation.

That's the whole system: look ahead, write it down, keep it small, share it once.

Our word, kept plainly

A grove this mixed holds together on a few shared commitments. These are ours.

Reciprocity

A gift for a gift — with the gods, with each other, and with the earth. We take nothing, from land or community, without giving something real back.

No gatekeeping

No one here grades your ancestry, your years of practice, or the price of your tools. Sincerity is the only credential this grove asks for.

Respect for closed doors

Some traditions are closed to outsiders by the people who carry them. We honor that the way we'd honor a neighbor's locked gate — without climbing it.

Leave no trace

Offerings that the earth can't take — plastic, glitter, synthetic ribbon — aren't offerings. Take your ritual home with you and leave the wild place wilder.

FrithFREETH

The old word for the peace inside a community. We disagree without contempt, correct without cruelty, and keep this grove safe for the newest walker in it.

Your path, your pace

Convert no one. Evangelize for nothing. If someone asks about your path, answer honestly and well — then let them walk their own.
